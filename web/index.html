            How this works
            • Build and reharmonise a chart, then play each bar and hear back whether the voicing under your hands says what the symbol says.
            • Play a line over the chart and see where every note lands. Arm a take and it keeps count across every bar you walk through.
            • Click a bar to work on it. Everything you play is checked against that bar - it is the one named beside Expecting. Arrow keys move along the chart.
            • Click a bar to solo over it. Every note you play is read against that bar's chord. Arrow keys move along the chart.
            • Click the same bar again for the substitutions you could put in its place - and, once a take has covered it, for what that take made of it.
            • Click the same bar again for the scales that fit it, and to be held to the one you pick - and, once a take has covered it, for what that take made of it.
            • Every bar keeps its own score - a coloured strip and a percentage - so the bar that got away is one you can see rather than read about.
            • Reharmonise the tune rewrites the whole chart to a named plan and says what it changed. Restore original puts it back.
            • Bring a chart in, or take one away. An iReal Pro link, a PDF or pasted chords in; a link or a printed page out.
            • The keys below stay down. Click them one at a time to build a chord - a voicing you are holding, not a piano you are playing. Clear keys lets go.
            • The keys do not stay down here. A line is played, not held: each note sounds and lets go, which is the opposite of chord practice on the same keyboard.
            • Show me one puts a voicing that works under your hands. Press it again for the next one, and again after that.
            • Show me a line plays one over this bar - and into the next when the chord moves - lighting the keys as it goes. Press again for another. Play it back and the take reads it like anything else you played.
            • Name it reads back whatever you are holding, with no chart in the way.
            • Guide tones marks the keys the next chord's 3rd and 7th are on, voiced from what you just played. One already under a finger keeps its badge - that one does not move.
            • A MIDI keyboard is better than clicking. Connect one under Practice.
            • Sustain holds what you played after the keys lift, as a pedal on a connected keyboard does. It appears once there is something to hold.
            • Every voicing gets a verdict. What it says about the bar, how completely it says it, and what is wrong with it if anything - a root under a rootless shape, two notes too close to be anything but mud.
            • Find a substitution by ear. If what you voiced spells one of the bar's substitutions, the dock says so rather than marking you down, and offers to write it into the bar.
            • The shape you are practising comes from Practice - shell, rootless, two-handed - and everything you play is judged against that one.
            • Chord tone, scale tone, approach note, outside. Every note is read against the bar it lands in, and the key lights in that colour as it sounds.
            • A note outside is not a mistake when you play it. It is open, and the next note decides it: step home and it reads as an approach note that counts as landing; go elsewhere and it settles as outside.
            • Take the target from both sides - above, below, then the note - and that is an enclosure, with a colour of its own. It counts as an approach note does.
            • A chord is read as voices, and as a chord. Each voice finds its own way home - sliding a voicing into the next is several lines at once - and what the notes came to is named under the one on top. Needs a MIDI keyboard.
            • The vocabulary you work out of comes from Practice - the modes, melodic minor, bebop, the pentatonics.
            • In time puts a clock behind the chart. The switch on the transport strip, under the chart's head. The chart moves on its own and the rolling bar keeps itself in view. The metre and tempo are on the strip beside it, and a tune imported in three arrives counted in three.
            • And turns this into comping. The keys stop latching - a comp is struck, not held - and each chord is read twice: what it said about the bar, and where it landed.
            • Start a take - the button on the strip, or the space bar. It counts a bar in, loops the range you set under Take setup, and stopping freezes the summary.
            • The tempo can climb, a few beats each time the loop comes round, up to a ceiling you choose. Take setup, and an option rather than a requirement.
            • The tune can change under you. Take setup → Reharmonise as you play reharmonises a bar, a few bars or the whole tune each chorus, going further out as it goes - then puts it back when the take stops.
            • Bring a band. Comping, above the chart, puts a piano behind you: two-handed rootless voicings, each led from the chord before and voiced a little differently each time round the form.
            • A bass walks under it, one note to the beat, and a drummer rides under both - in time only, since the kit keeps time rather than reading the chart.
            • Pick a style and a sound for each. The style decides where chords fall and how long they ring. The grand and both basses are recordings.
            • In time they play with the clock; static, each bar sounds as you land on it.
            • No piano behind chord practice. Bass and drums play, but a comping piano would be playing the part you are being asked to play. In solo practice it comes in.
